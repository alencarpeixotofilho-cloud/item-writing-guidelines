# 2. Writing the stem (clinical vignette)

The stem contains **all the information needed to answer** and nothing that
belongs in the options.

## 2.1 Recommended order

1. Age, sex and setting (*"A 34-year-old woman comes to the clinic…"*)
2. Chief complaint and duration
3. History of present illness
4. Past history, medications, family and social history (only what matters)
5. Physical examination, beginning with vital signs
6. Results of initial investigations

Presenting information in the order a clinician would gather it makes the
item read naturally and reduces cognitive load unrelated to the construct.

## 2.2 Do

* Give **findings, not interpretations**: write *"temperature 39.2 °C"*, not
  *"febrile"*; *"serum sodium 124 mEq/L"*, not *"hyponatremia"* — unless
  interpreting the finding is not what the item tests.
* Include reference ranges for less common laboratory values.
* Keep the vignette as long as needed and as short as possible.

## 2.3 Avoid

* **Window dressing**: irrelevant detail added only to make the item look harder.
* **Red herrings** designed to trick rather than to test discrimination.
* Revealing the answer in the stem (e.g. naming the disease you then ask about).
* Ambiguous pronouns or time references (*"recently"*, *"some time ago"*).

## 2.4 Example

> A 58-year-old man comes to the emergency department 40 minutes after the
> onset of crushing substernal chest pain radiating to the jaw, with
> diaphoresis and nausea. He has hypertension and smokes one pack of
> cigarettes daily. Blood pressure is 96/60 mm Hg and pulse is 52/min. The
> electrocardiogram shows ST-segment elevation in leads II, III and aVF.

Every sentence contributes to the decision being tested.
