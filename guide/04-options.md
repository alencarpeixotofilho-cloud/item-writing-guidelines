# 4. Writing the options

## 4.1 The key

* There is **one best answer**, defensible with current evidence and accepted
  by content experts without debate.
* The key is not the longest, most detailed or most qualified option.

## 4.2 Distractors

Good distractors are **plausible to examinees who lack the knowledge** and
clearly wrong to those who have it. Sources of plausible distractors:

* common misconceptions and frequent errors observed in practice or teaching;
* conditions in the differential diagnosis of the vignette;
* the right answer to a *different* but related question.

Three to four functional distractors are usually enough. A distractor chosen
by fewer than ~5% of examinees is **non-functional** and adds reading time
without adding measurement (see the companion project
[`item-analysis-psychometrics`](https://github.com/alencarpeixotofilho-cloud/item-analysis-psychometrics)).

## 4.3 Homogeneity

All options should be:

* of the **same category** (all diagnoses, all drugs, all mechanisms);
* of **similar length and level of detail**;
* **grammatically consistent** with the lead-in;
* in a **logical order** (numbers ascending, anatomical order, alphabetical).

## 4.4 Avoid

* *All of the above* / *None of the above* — reward partial knowledge and
  test-taking strategy.
* **Absolute terms** (*always, never, only*) — test-wise examinees eliminate them.
* **Vague frequency terms** (*often, usually, rarely*) — interpreted differently
  by different readers.
* **Overlapping options** (e.g. "< 3.5" and "< 3.0") and **convergence cues**
  (the key shares elements with most distractors).
* **Combined options** ("A and C", "I, II and III").
